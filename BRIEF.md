# Data Detective: Can You Trust This Claim? — interactive education website for age 15

Build a working educational website, not an idea list, mockup, slide deck or requirements-only response. Target approximately age 15, allowing individual differences in experience and ability. Session target: A focused 10–15 minute investigation with optional deeper exploration.

## Learning outcomes
Explain how graph presentation can alter perceived effect size; identify a sampling limitation; distinguish correlation from causal evidence; and write or assemble a cautious, evidence-based conclusion. Teach scrutiny rather than blanket distrust of data.

## Required activities
1. Graph framing: compare two views of the same synthetic dataset, manipulate the vertical-axis range, and identify what changed in appearance versus what stayed numerically identical. Explain why context matters; do not claim every non-zero axis is automatically deceptive.
2. Sampling investigation: draw or select samples from a documented synthetic population using at least one plausibly biased and one less biased method. Compare sample composition and summaries with the population. Explain why a larger biased sample need not fix bias.
3. Correlation challenge: explore two related variables and a plausible third-variable explanation. Let learners stratify or compare groups, then choose and justify an appropriate conclusion. End with a new fictional headline combining at least two of these issues.

## Age-specific experience
Use a mature, respectful interface, readable charts and concise editorial copy. Provide an evidence notebook or equivalent summary of observations. Open-ended answers may remain entirely local; automated scoring must not pretend to understand arbitrary prose. If free text is used, provide a self-review checklist or exemplar reasoning. Accept reasonable nuanced interpretations that fit the data, not just a single slogan.

## Model and content requirements
Bundle original synthetic datasets with labels, units, provenance notes and a data dictionary. Identify every headline/person/organisation as fictional where applicable. Document how the data were constructed and avoid implying real scientific findings. Calculations and charts must use the same data. Avoid medical, political or personally sensitive real-world claims; no collection of the learner's demographics.

## Concrete acceptance examples
- Changing an axis changes the rendering but not underlying values or computed statistics.
- Every chart has labelled axes/units, a useful accessible table and an understandable reset.
- The biased and less biased sampling procedures match their documented definitions.
- Repeated sampling is reproducible in tests, or uses supplied fixed test cases.
- The correlation activity explicitly states that association alone does not establish causation.
- The final response asks for a claim, supporting observation and limitation, with useful feedback.
